Pronunciation & Phonetics

Arabic has sounds English simply doesn't — most of them made in the throat. Here is exactly where each one is produced, and every diacritic that changes how a letter sounds.

Points of articulation (makhārij), throat to lips

Traditional Arabic phonetics organizes the alphabet by where in the mouth and throat each sound is produced — a genuinely physical sequence, not an alphabetical one. Working through it in order, from deepest to shallowest, is the fastest way to build a working "throat map":

#ZoneLettersNotes

The throat letters, practically speaking

These six sounds (ء ه ع ح غ خ) cause the most trouble for English speakers because none of them exist in English. A practical approach:

  • ه (h) — identical to English "h". No difficulty here.
  • ء (hamza) — the catch in your throat in the middle of English "uh-oh". Every Arabic word technically starts with either a hamza or a consonant; you never truly "start with a vowel".
  • ح (ḥāʾ) — whisper the word "who" while tightening your throat as if fogging a mirror, but keep it voiceless. It should feel like friction, not a cough.
  • ع (ʿayn) — the same throat constriction as ح, but voiced (add your vocal cords). Often described as a "strangled a" — many learners find it easier to feel than to describe. Practice by gently gagging on an "aaa" sound with the throat tightened.
  • خ (khāʾ) — like clearing your throat gently, or the "ch" in Scottish "loch".
  • غ (ghayn) — the voiced twin of خ; close to a French uvular "r", a soft gargle.

Emphatic vs. plain consonants

Arabic has four "emphatic" (also called velarized or "heavy") consonants, each paired with a plain counterpart. The emphatic version is produced by pulling the back of the tongue up and back toward the soft palate while making the sound — this also darkens the color of nearby vowels, making a following "a" sound closer to "aw" and giving emphatic words a noticeably heavier, rounder quality.

PlainEmphaticWhat changes
س (s)ص (ṣ)Tongue pulls back; following vowels sound darker.
د (d)ض (ḍ)Same shift; ض is the sound classical scholars called uniquely Arabic.
ت (t)ط (ṭ)Heavier, more forceful release than plain ت.
ذ (dh)ظ (ẓ)Same interdental placement as ذ, but velarized.
Quick test
Say "Sam" then say "Saum" while pulling your tongue back and rounding slightly — the second is roughly the emphatic feel. Minimal pairs like سيف sayf ("sword") vs. صيف ṣayf ("summer") exist only because of this single articulatory difference.

Short vowels, long vowels, and the marks (ḥarakāt)

Standard Arabic script written for adults and fluent readers normally omits short-vowel marks entirely — readers infer them from context and grammar, the way English speakers don't mark stress. Learning materials (and this guide) add them back in as small marks above or below the letter:

MarkNameSoundExample

بِkasrashort "i"كِتَاب kitāb

بً بٍ بٌtanwīnadds a trailing "n" (an, in, un)كِتَابٌ kitābun ("a book")

Long vowels aren't diacritics at all — they're full letters (ا, و, ي) used to stretch a short vowel to roughly double its length: fatḥa + ا = ā, ḍamma + و = ū, kasra + ي = ī. Vowel length is phonemic in Arabic, meaning it can be the only difference between two otherwise-unrelated words — كَتَبَ kataba ("he wrote") is unrelated to a hypothetical kaataba only by coincidence of this system, but plenty of real pairs like جَمَل jamal ("camel") and جَمِيل jamīl ("beautiful") are distinguished by exactly this kind of vowel change.

Hamza and its carrier seats

Hamza (ء) represents the glottal stop and is technically a consonant, not a diacritic — but because it has no fixed tall stem of its own, it needs to "sit" on a carrier letter, chosen by the surrounding vowels:

  • أ — hamza on alif (used when the surrounding vowel is a/u): أَكَلَ akala ("he ate")
  • إ — hamza under alif (used with an i-vowel): إِسْم ism ("name")
  • ؤ — hamza on wāw (used near u/long-ū): سُؤَال suʾāl ("question")
  • ئ — hamza on yāʾ, dotless (used near an i-vowel): مِائَة miʾa ("hundred")
  • ء — hamza on the line by itself, typically between two long vowels or at the end of a word after a long vowel: شَيْء shayʾ ("thing")
The seat is not the sound
Whichever letter hamza sits on, the sound is still just a glottal stop — أ, إ, ؤ, and ئ are not four different consonants, they're one consonant with four different spellings chosen by context. This trips up almost every learner at some point; when in doubt, it helps to look the exact spelling up rather than guess.

Word stress

Arabic stress is largely predictable from syllable weight rather than memorized per word: stress tends to fall on the last syllable that is "heavy" — closed (ends in a consonant) or containing a long vowel — counting from the end of the word, skipping the final syllable itself in most dialects of Modern Standard Arabic recitation. In practice: don't stress words the way you'd guess from English — listen for which syllable carries a long vowel or is closed by two consonants, and lean on that one.

One honest note on dialects

Everything on this page describes Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) — the shared, formal register used in writing, news, and education across the Arab world. Spoken dialects (Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, Maghrebi, and others) diverge from MSA in pronunciation and grammar, sometimes significantly — for example ج is pronounced "g" in Egyptian Arabic, and ق is often dropped to a glottal stop in Levantine urban dialects. Learning MSA first gives you a stable reference point that every dialect is a variation on.
