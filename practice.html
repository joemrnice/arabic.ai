Frequently asked, specifically

They share the exact same base skeleton — only the dots differ (one below for ب, two above for ت, three above for ث). Arabic reuses letter skeletons across families this way; see the full dot breakdown.
Not in normal adult writing — newspapers, novels, and everyday text drop them entirely. They mainly appear in children's books, the Qurʾān, dictionaries, and language-learning material, exactly where this guide uses them: as training wheels for reading.
ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) only ever occurs at the end of a word and marks feminine gender; it's pronounced as a light "a" in pausal speech or "-at" before a following word in careful/connected speech. ه (hāʾ) is a full, ordinary consonant "h" that can appear anywhere in a word. They only look similar in final position — compare their isolated forms and you'll see ة keeps two dots.
Roughly: an i-vowel nearby pulls hamza onto ي (dotless, ئ); a u-vowel pulls it onto و (ؤ); an a-vowel or a word-initial position puts it on ا (أgoing up for a/u, إ going down for i). At the end of a word after a long vowel, or between two long vowels, it often sits alone on the line (ء). See the full breakdown on the phonetics page — and when genuinely unsure, look the word up rather than guess, since this is one of the few areas even native speakers occasionally disagree on.
Text and letters flow right to left, but multi-digit numbers are written left to right, the same direction as in English — so a phone number or a price reads in the "wrong" direction relative to the rest of the line. Your eye genuinely has to switch direction mid-sentence.
ش is a "sun letter". When the definite article ال is attached to a word starting with one of the 14 sun letters, the ل silently assimilates into the following consonant, doubling it instead of being pronounced itself. It's still spelled with the ل — only the pronunciation changes. Full list on the alphabet page.
Almost universally ع (ʿayn) — a voiced pharyngeal fricative with no equivalent anywhere in English. ح (its voiceless twin) and ض (the emphatic ḍād) are the usual runners-up. All three are covered with practice tips on the phonetics page.
It's simply the fixed word order in Arabic — noun first, then any adjectives describing it, each one agreeing in gender, number, case, and definiteness. There's no deeper trick to it beyond memorizing the order is reversed from English.
Because the adjective has to agree with the noun in definiteness too, not just gender and number: البيت الكبير, literally "the-house the-big". Drop the article from only the adjective and the same two words become a sentence instead of a phrase — see adjective agreement.
No — the majority of Arabic plurals are "broken", meaning the internal vowel pattern changes instead of (or as well as) adding a suffix: كتاب → كتب. Regular suffix-based "sound" plurals exist too, but broken plurals are the norm, not the exception. See number.
Because non-human plurals take feminine-singular agreement regardless of how many items there are — a rule with no real English analogue. الكتب كبيرة uses the singular feminine adjective كبيرة, not a plural form. Covered in the grammar page's number section.
For numbers 3–10, the number's grammatical gender flips relative to the noun it counts: counting masculine nouns uses the feminine-looking form of the number (ending in ة), and counting feminine nouns uses the masculine-looking form. It looks backwards because, relative to every other agreement rule in Arabic, it is. See numbers.
A short vowel (or tanwīn) added to the end of a noun or verb marking its grammatical role — subject, object, possessor, and so on. It's mostly silent in casual conversation but pronounced in formal reading, news broadcasting, and Qurʾānic recitation. See the case system.
Through iḍāfa: two nouns placed directly next to each other, with the possessed noun first (losing its own ال/tanwīn) and the possessor second in the genitive case. بيت الرجل, literally "house-of the-man". See iḍāfa.
It's real but bounded: the formal default for verbal sentences is Verb–Subject–Object, SVO is also common and fully grammatical, and elements can be fronted for emphasis — but adjectives always follow their noun and the possessor in iḍāfa always comes second. See word order.
لَمْ negates the past by pairing with the present-tense verb put into the jussive mood (لَمْ أَكْتُبْ, "I did not write") — a genuine historical quirk of the grammar, not an error. See negating verbs.
The root is a set of (usually three) consonants carrying a core, abstract meaning — ك ت ب relates to writing. The pattern is the scaffold of vowels, prefixes, and doubled letters wrapped around that root to produce a specific word: kataba (he wrote), kitāb (book), maktab (office), kātib (writer) are the same root in four different patterns. See the home page and verb forms.
This guide teaches MSA, the shared formal register understood across every Arabic-speaking country and used in writing, news, and education. It's the most useful starting point precisely because every regional dialect is a variation on it — once MSA is solid, picking up a dialect for a specific country is much faster than the reverse.
It's not stress or emphasis — it's a genuinely doubled consonant, held for roughly twice the normal duration, the way Italian doubles consonants in words like "pizza". مُدَرِّس (mudarris, "teacher") has an audibly longer ر than a single-ر word would. See vowels & marks.
Six letters — ا د ذ ر ز و — historically never developed a connecting stroke on their left side, so whatever comes after them starts a fresh, unconnected shape even mid-word. It's a fixed, memorizable list rather than a rule you can derive. See letter forms.
Fairly predictable from syllable weight — stress tends to land on the last "heavy" syllable (closed, or containing a long vowel) rather than being memorized per word the way English stress often has to be. See word stress.
Emphatic consonants (ص ض ط ظ) pull the back of the tongue toward the soft palate, which also darkens nearby vowels — a fully separate phoneme from their plain counterparts (س د ت ذ), not just a stylistic variation. Minimal pairs like سيف/صيف exist purely because of this. See emphatics.
No — every noun is masculine or feminine, with no neuter category at all. Objects and abstract concepts still have to be assigned a gender, which is why gender has to be learned per-word for non-human nouns. See gender.
A traditional numbered classification (I–X) of the derivational patterns a root can take, each shifting meaning in a predictable direction — causative, reciprocal, reflexive, and so on. Every dictionary entry lists which forms a root is attested in. Full table on the verbs page.
