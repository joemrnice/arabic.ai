Sentence Structure

Arabic runs two parallel sentence types side by side, tolerates a flexible word order English can't, and negates each type differently.

Nominal sentences (jumla ismiyya) — no verb required

Arabic doesn't need a verb "to be" in the present tense at all. A topic followed directly by a description is already a complete, grammatical sentence:

ArabicWord-for-wordMeaning
البَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ
al-baytu kabīrun		the-house big"The house is big."
هِيَ طَبِيبَةٌ
hiya ṭabībatun		she doctor"She is a doctor."

The two parts have names: mubtadaʾ (topic — what we're talking about) and khabar (predicate — what's said about it). The khabar can be an adjective (as above), another noun, or even a whole prepositional phrase: الكِتَابُ عَلَى الطَاوِلَةِ al-kitābu ʿalā ṭ-ṭāwilati ("the book is on the table").

Verbal sentences (jumla fiʿliyya) — verb comes first

When a sentence does contain a verb, the traditional and most formal order is Verb–Subject–Object (VSO) — the reverse of English's Subject-Verb-Object:

Arabic (VSO)Word-for-wordEnglish (SVO)
كَتَبَ الوَلَدُ الرِسَالَةَ
kataba l-waladu r-risālata		wrote the-boy the-letter"The boy wrote the letter."
Why VSO doesn't cause confusion
Because nouns carry case endings, the sentence stays unambiguous even with the subject and object swapped in position — al-waladu is nominative (the doer) regardless of where it sits. In practice, spoken Arabic and journalistic writing very often use SVO too; both orders are grammatical, and the choice shifts emphasis rather than meaning.

Forming questions

TypeWordExample
Yes/noهَلْ / أَهَلْ أَنْتَ طَالِبٌ؟ hal anta ṭālibun? "Are you a student?"
Whatمَا / مَاذَامَا اسْمُكَ؟ mā ismuka? "What is your name?"

Whereأَيْنَأَيْنَ البَيْتُ؟ ayna l-baytu? "Where is the house?"
Whenمَتَىمَتَى وَصَلْتَ؟ matā waṣalta? "When did you arrive?"
Whyلِمَاذَالِمَاذَا؟ limādhā? "Why?"

Note كم forces the counted noun into the singular accusative (kitāban, not kutub) — one of several small quirks around Arabic numbers covered on the vocabulary page.

Negating each sentence type

Sentence typeNegatorExample
Nominal, presentلَيْسَالبَيْتُ لَيْسَ كَبِيرًا al-baytu laysa kabīran "The house isn't big"
Verbal, pastلَمْ + jussiveلَمْ يَكْتُبْ lam yaktub "He didn't write"
Verbal, presentلَالَا يَكْتُبُ lā yaktubu "He doesn't write"

لَيْسَ (laysa) is grammatically a verb ("to not be") even though it negates a verbless sentence — it conjugates like a past-tense verb (laysa, laysat, lastu…) but is always translated as a present-tense "is not/am not/are not".

How much can word order actually move?

Beyond VSO and SVO, Arabic allows fronting almost any element for emphasis — object-first sentences exist for contrast ("the letter, the boy wrote"), and question words always move to the very front regardless of their grammatical role. What stays fixed: adjectives always follow their noun, and the possessor in an iḍāfa always comes second. Those two rules almost never bend.
