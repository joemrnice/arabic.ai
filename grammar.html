Grammar Deep-Dive

Nouns, gender, number, case endings, possession, and agreement — the machinery that decides how words change shape around each other.

Gender: only two, and mostly predictable

Every Arabic noun is either masculine or feminine — there is no neuter. The single strongest signal is the letter ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) at the end of a word: nouns ending in it are almost always feminine. Human nouns generally follow biological sex; most other feminine nouns are simply feminine by convention and have to be learned, though a few patterns help (cities and countries, paired body parts, and most abstract nouns tend to be feminine).

Number: singular, dual, and plural

Arabic marks three numbers, not two — this is one of its most distinctive features for English speakers, who only distinguish singular/plural.

  • Singular — one of something: كِتَاب kitāb ("a book")
  • Dual — exactly two, marked with the suffix ـَانِ / ـَيْنِ: كِتَابَانِ kitābāni ("two books")
  • Plural — three or more. Arabic plurals split into two systems:
    • Sound plurals — a regular suffix added to the singular. Masculine sound plural: مُعَلِّمُونَ muʿallimūna ("teachers, m."). Feminine sound plural: مُعَلِّمَاتٌ muʿallimātun ("teachers, f.").
    • Broken plurals — the internal vowel pattern changes instead, the way English "mouse → mice" breaks the normal "-s" rule, except in Arabic this irregular-looking process is actually the majority pattern for plurals: كِتَاب → كُتُب (kitāb → kutub), قَلَم → أَقْلَام (qalam → aqlām, "pen(s)").
The rule that surprises everyone
Non-human plurals (objects, animals, abstract things — anything that isn't a person) are grammatically treated as feminine singular for agreement purposes, no matter how many there are. "The books are big" is literally الكُتُبُ كَبِيرَةٌ — al-kutubu kabīratun, using the feminine singular adjective kabīra, not a plural adjective.

Definiteness: ال and its absence

Arabic has a definite article, ال (al-, "the"), prefixed directly onto the noun with no space — but no indefinite article ("a/an"). A bare noun is automatically indefinite. In careful/formal speech, indefinite nouns also take a final tanwīn (see the phonetics page) that spoken colloquial Arabic usually drops.

IndefiniteDefinite
بَيْتٌ
baytun — "a house"		البَيْتُ
al-baytu — "the house"

Remember: if the noun starts with a sun letter, the ل of ال is silent and the following consonant doubles instead — spelled the same, pronounced differently.

The case system (iʿrāb)

Formal Arabic marks a noun's grammatical role with a short vowel ending — a feature mostly silent in casual speech but fully present in careful reading, news broadcasting, and the Qurʾān. There are three cases:

CaseUsed forTypical endingExample
Nominative (marfūʿ)The subject of a sentence-u / -unالوَلَدُ al-waladu "the boy" (as subject)
Accusative (manṣūb)The direct object-a / -anرَأَيْتُ الوَلَدَ raʾaytu l-walada "I saw the boy"
Genitive (majrūr)After a preposition, or as the second noun in iḍāfa-i / -inفِي البَيْتِ fī l-bayti "in the house"

These endings are why Arabic word order can be flexible — the case ending, not position, tells you who did what to whom, similar in spirit (though not mechanism) to Latin or German case endings.

Iḍāfa: the possessive construction

Arabic has no separate word for "of" or a possessive apostrophe. Instead, two nouns are placed directly next to each other in a fixed structure called iḍāfa ("addition"): the first noun (the thing possessed) drops its definite article and any tanwīn, and the second noun (the possessor) takes the genitive case and carries the definiteness for the whole phrase.

ArabicLiteral structureMeaning
بَيْتُ الرَّجُلِ
baytu r-rajuli		house-of the-man (genitive)"the man's house"
كِتَابُ طَالِبٍ
kitābu ṭālibin		book-of a-student"a student's book"

Chains longer than two nouns are possible — "the door of the house of the man" — each link staying genitive except the very first noun.

Adjectives: they follow, and they agree

Adjectives come after the noun they describe (the reverse of English) and must agree with it in gender, number, case, and definiteness:

ArabicWord-for-wordMeaning
بَيْتٌ كَبِيرٌ
baytun kabīrun		house-indef. big-indef."a big house"
البَيْتُ الكَبِيرُ
al-baytu l-kabīru		the-house the-big"the big house" — both words take ال
Where the "sentence" hides
Drop the article from just the adjective — البَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ (al-baytu kabīrun) — and the phrase becomes a full sentence: "the house is big". Definiteness mismatch is what turns a description into a predicate. See nominal sentences.

Attached pronouns don't just mark possession on nouns — the same suffixes attach to verbs to mark a direct object (رَأَيْتُهُ raʾaytuhu, "I saw him") and to prepositions (لَهُ lahu, "to/for him").

Root and pattern: the grammar behind the vocabulary

The concept introduced on the home page is itself a grammar topic: Arabic morphology layers a fixed vowel pattern (called a wazn, "measure") onto a consonantal root. The pattern maCCaC tends to produce a place noun; the pattern CāCiC tends to produce an active participant/doer. Once you recognize a handful of patterns, unfamiliar words become guessable rather than opaque.
