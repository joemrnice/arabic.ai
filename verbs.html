Verbs & Conjugation

Arabic verbs are built, not memorized one at a time: a three-letter root plus a pattern of vowels and affixes gives you tense, subject, and often meaning itself.

Two core tenses (really: two aspects)

Arabic verbs are traditionally described as having two main conjugations, closer to grammatical aspect than to English tense:

  • Past / perfective (al-māḍī) — a completed action. Built by adding suffixes to the root: كَتَبْـتُ katab-tu ("I wrote").
  • Present / imperfective (al-muḍāriʿ) — an ongoing or habitual action. Built by adding both a prefix and a suffix: أَ-كْتُبُ a-ktubu ("I write / am writing").

Future is formed by prefixing سَـ (sa-) or سَوْفَ (sawfa) onto the present tense — there's no separate future conjugation to learn.

The root-and-pattern engine

Almost every Arabic verb reduces to a bare 3-letter root carrying an abstract core meaning, and a pattern (vowels, doubled letters, prefixes) that turns that meaning into a specific verb, and by extension into related nouns and adjectives.

The ten verb forms

Classical Arabic grammar organizes derived verbs into ten numbered "forms" (I is the plain root verb; II–X each add a specific prefix/infix pattern that shifts the meaning in a predictable direction — causative, reflexive, reciprocal, and so on). This is a real numbered sequence used throughout every Arabic dictionary, so the numbering below is functional, not decorative.

Why this is worth learning early
Recognize the root س ل م once, and سَلَام (salām, "peace"), إِسْلَام (islām, Form IV verbal noun — literally "submission"), and مُسْلِم (muslim, Form IV active participle — "one who submits") stop being three unrelated vocabulary items and become one idea viewed from three angles.

Pattern to notice
Past tense is built by adding a suffix to the root (كَتَبْ + تُ, كَتَبْ + تَ...). Present tense wraps the root in both a prefix and often a suffix (أَ + كتب + ُ, تَ + كتب + ِينَ...). Once this shape is internalized, you can conjugate almost any Form-I root the same way — try it live in the conjugation drill project.

The imperative
